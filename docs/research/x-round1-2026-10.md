## 2. Top findings per weak question

**end-user-price-band (the strongest new finding).** NameBio prices its sister company NamePull's portfolio of about 70,000 names, and it ran two tests on it:
- Test 1: the bottom 30% of the portfolio was priced at $995-$1,795. NameBio moved all of those names to $2,195. The number of sales did not fall (still 14% of all sales) and revenue rose 38%.
- Test 2 (reverse): about 2,000 names priced at $1,888-$2,888 were cut to $988. Sell-through did not change.

Darpan Munjal (Atom's CEO) says the same for brandable names: tests showed no difference in sell-through between $1,499 and $1,999. Grades: B, B (linked as one cluster) and C. Indirect, because these are large brandable and hand-reg portfolios. Implication for us: a $1,488 Buy Now probably sits below the price range where buyers react to price. Raising default Buy Now prices toward $1,988-$2,488 may cost few sales. That is worth a CR or a pick-proof-style test before any rule change.

Anecdotes of auction-bought names resold to end users sit at about $1,500-$7,500, for names bought at $70-$225. These are D grade (weight 0, survivorship). One is C: Brad Mugford bought a name for $225 at auction in 2019, sold it for $7,500 in 2023, then won it back at auction for $674. One D anecdote is a $155 auction name dumped for $299 after 2 years.

**sell-through-yearly.** Doron Vermaat sold **38 of about 1,600 names in 2025 (2.4%)**. That is a whole-portfolio count, B grade, median sale $4,995. NameBio sees about 28 reported sales a day against about 24 million listed names, and says large portfolios are often below 1%. Shane Cultra reports 3.5% (C grade, self-reported, credibility 4). With the existing Hawkes figures (0.5% and 1.1%), the range is 0.5-3.5%. All of it is indirect: it comes from portfolios of thousands of names, not 40 hand-picked S8 names. Michael Cyger's public $500 challenge ledger shows 38 of 41 cheap GoDaddy closeout and auction names sold within a year, but mostly **to investors at $99-$888**. That is wholesale flipping, a different business from ours.

**signal-auction-bid-count.** Brad Mugford (C) says GoDaddy and Dynadot bids are run up by pairs of bidders who then don't pay. So a bid count is not clean proof of demand. The Digiventures post is the original X source of the "150 lost GoDaddy auctions: 64% went to HugeDomains and PerfectDomain, only one went to an end user" study. It is now linked as the original that DNW echoed (one cluster, as before).

**s8-auctions-vs-closeouts, s8-max-bid-share.** There is **no new usable evidence** on either. Nobody on X posts a comparison of auction-bought and closeout-bought sell-through, or a max-bid rule with numbers. The only ratio we have is the one Mugford anecdote: auction prices of 3-9% of the later end-user price. It is recorded as `qualifies`, not as a max-bid value.

**line1-hand-regs-worth-buying.** One new piece: Sullivan's 22 Oct article (found through his X post). It is an argument with no new data: a hand-reg is a renewal commitment, so register only names worth 3 years of fees. Recorded as `qualifies`.

**signal-keyword-volume-cpc.** Nothing found on X.

**Not tied to a question (marketplaces).** NameBio says 60-65% of sales come through the Afternic and Sedo networks. One investor's sales halved when he delisted. This supports keeping every name in Afternic's network.

## 4. What X is and isn't good for

**Good for:**
- Finding leads to originals.
- Practitioner pricing experiments that are published nowhere else (NameBio's tests).
- Whole-portfolio yearly numbers that some investors post in January.
- Auction-integrity warnings.

**Not good for:**
- Sell-through by source (auction vs closeout vs hand-reg).
- Max-bid rules.
- Anything statistical about our narrow case.

Most "sold" posts are survivorship (wins only, often without the price). Many recent results are .si hype and promotion. Some "sold" posts are really live auctions.

Access: the search-engine route is nearly useless, because posts return 402. The logged-in route works, but it can't be automated legally for free (the X API is paid; see `kb-outside-sources-free-legal`). Use it as an occasional manual lead source: once a quarter, plus January for the yearly results posts.
